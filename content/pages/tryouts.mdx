## What a tryout is actually like

A tryout is how a program decides who is on a team. That can be one evening of drills and a scrimmage, a weekend, or a few sessions in a row. Some programs call it an evaluation or ask your child to come to practice. The name is less important than the format.

You should be told, in plain language, what the day will look like, what the coaches are looking at, and when you’ll hear back. If those answers are fuzzy, treat that as useful information. [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) has questions worth asking before you get attached to a roster spot.

## Getting ready

The most useful preparation is ordinary: sleep, a normal meal, knowing the time and the place, and having the right shoes and gear packed the night before. This is not the week to overhaul a skill, and it is not the night to stay up talking about what a spot on this team would mean.

Tell your child what you know about the format, and that they don’t have to perform a version of themselves they think a coach wants. Your job is logistics and a calm presence. Their job is to show up and play.

## The day itself

Arrive with enough time to check in without rushing. Then let the coaches run the session. Watching is fine; coaching from the sideline is not, and comparing your child to someone else’s out loud helps no one.

When it’s over, get to the car before you start the recap. A short “How did that feel?” is enough in the moment. Save the longer talk for home, after everyone has had a little air.

## The wait

Hearing back often takes longer than the email promised. Silence is hard, and it is not always a verdict. Try not to let the group chat, or a rumor in the parking lot, run your house for a week.

If the timeline passes and you’ve heard nothing, it is reasonable to send one polite question. Then wait for the actual answer.

## Offers, and the pressure to commit

An offer can feel like a relief, and then immediately like a countdown. You do not have to accept in the parking lot, or in the same hour the email arrives.

A yes is a season, not a compliment. Before you pay a deposit, you still get to look at the schedule, the cost, and whether this is the program you thought it was. [Costs](/costs) and [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) are for that part. If a program will not give a family a short window to talk it through at home, that is information too.

## If the answer is no

A no is about this team, this season, this moment. It is not a verdict on your child, and it is not the end of the sport unless you decide it is.

Give the feeling some room before you start solving. Rec still exists. Another program may be a better fit. Waiting a season is allowed. The useful conversation happens later, at home: what they want to do next, not how to prove the coaches wrong.

## Talking with your child

Before the tryout, keep the stakes small. After the tryout, listen first. If they made a team, talk about what the season will actually ask of them, not only how proud you are. If they didn’t, don’t rush to fill the silence with a speech about resilience, and don’t criticize the coaches in a way that makes your child responsible for your disappointment.

The question underneath all of this is the same one as before: do they want to keep going, or are the adults the ones who need this to work out?

## Before you go

<Checklist>

- Confirm the time, place, what to bring, and when you’ll hear back.
- Ask what the tryout will actually look like — drills, scrimmage, one day or several.
- Keep the night before ordinary: pack, eat, sleep.
- Agree on how you’ll talk after, at home, not in the parking lot.
- If an offer comes, take time to review cost, schedule, and fit before you pay a deposit.
- If the answer is no, give it a little time before you decide the next move together.

</Checklist>
