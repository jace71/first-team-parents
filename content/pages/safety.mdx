## Coach-athlete boundaries

A coach can be close, demanding, and kind, and still keep a clear line. The line is what keeps a child safe, and it should be visible to other adults.

In a well-run program, one-on-one time in a closed room, a car, or a locker room is not the normal way of doing business. Messages to a child are not a private channel that cuts parents out. Physical contact is public, tied to the sport or to first aid, and easy to explain. Special secrets, exclusive relationships, or a coach who wants to be the only adult in your child’s sport life are reasons to pay attention and to ask questions.

You should be able to get a plain answer about the program’s rules for contact, messaging, locker rooms, and travel. If those questions are treated as an insult, that is information. [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) is about that kind of clarity in general; it matters most here.

## Travel and lodging

Overnight trips need a plan you can hear out loud before you say yes. Who is in charge. How rooms are assigned. Whether a parent can attend. What happens if your child is uncomfortable at two in the morning.

Adults should not share rooms with other people’s children. Older and younger athletes rooming together, unsupervised free time in a hotel, or a last-minute change to “just ride with the coach” are things to question, not to wave through because everyone is tired. If a program cannot tell you its travel and lodging rules, do not assume it has them.

## Injuries, heat, and head injuries

This is not a diagnosis guide, and it is not a substitute for a doctor. If your child is hurt, follow a health care provider’s advice and the program’s written protocols. You do not need a coach’s permission to get your child checked.

If something seems wrong — pain, dizziness, confusion, a child who is unusually quiet, sick, or not themselves — sit them out. “They want to keep playing” is not the same as being okay to keep playing. Return to practice or games should follow the doctor and the protocol, not a good half-hour later or a parent’s hope that it wasn’t serious.

Heat needs the same seriousness. Hot days require water, rest, and a coach who will stop. If your child feels faint, sick, confused, or stops sweating, get them out of the heat and get medical help.

A bump, blow, or jolt to the head — or a hit to the body that snaps the head — is a reason to sit your child out and contact a health care provider. Do not let them “shake it off” and return the same day on a hunch. The CDC’s [HEADS UP](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/index.html) pages have parent-level information on concussions. They can help you understand what to watch for. They do not clear a child to play. A health care provider does that.

## What to ask a program

You are allowed to ask these questions before you commit, and again before a trip. A program that already takes safety seriously will not be surprised by them.

<Checklist>

- What are your rules for coach-athlete contact, messaging, locker rooms, and one-on-one situations?
- Do staff and volunteers have background checks? How often, and who is included — coaches, assistants, drivers, overnight chaperones?
- What safety training is required, and of whom?
- How are rooms, curfews, and supervision handled on overnight trips?
- What is the protocol for injuries, heat, and possible concussions, and who is allowed to sit a child out?
- If a parent or child has a concern, whom do we tell, and what happens next?

</Checklist>

The [U.S. Center for SafeSport](https://uscenterforsafesport.org/) publishes training and policies used across Olympic and Paralympic sport, and some programs outside that system use the same training or similar rules. Ask what your program actually requires. The Center’s site also has resources for parents. A certificate on a wall is not the same thing as a culture you can see, and not every club or travel team falls under the Center’s formal process.

## How to speak up

If something feels off, you do not have to wait for a perfect speech, or for proof that would satisfy a stranger. Write down what you saw or what your child told you. You can start with the coach or a director. If that is not safe, or it goes nowhere, go to the club, the league, or the organization that oversees the program.

If you believe a child is in immediate danger, contact local law enforcement. For concerns about abuse or misconduct, the program should be able to tell you how to report. The U.S. Center for SafeSport site also explains how reporting works in the settings where the Center has a role.

You can take your child out of a situation while you figure out the next step. A program that punishes a parent or a child for asking a safety question is telling you what you need to know.
