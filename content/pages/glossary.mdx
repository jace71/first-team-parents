This page is a plain-language list of the ones you’ll hear most. The definitions are general, not official. When a word matters most in another chapter, you’ll see a pointer there. If a flyer or a sideline uses a word that still isn’t clear, ask what they mean by it here.

## Kinds of programs

These labels get used as if they were precise. They aren’t. [Start here](/start-here) is the fuller map; this is the short version.

<Cards>

<Card title="Academy" id="academy">

A training-focused setup, sometimes attached to a club or a facility. It may be a team, a training track, or mostly classes.

Ask whether it’s a team, a training program, or both — and what “academy” means here. [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) covers how to tell.

</Card>

<Card title="Club" id="club">

An organized program, often with paid coaches, regular training, and a path through age groups. It can be year-round or multi-season. Soccer, volleyball, basketball, and lacrosse use “club” a lot; other sports may use “travel” for something that looks the same.

Club can look a lot like travel: tryouts, higher fees, more hours. Some clubs also field rec or developmental teams. Ask what this specific team actually does. See [Start here](/start-here).

</Card>

<Card title="Developmental team" id="developmental-team">

A team inside a club or program that is meant to be a step toward a more competitive roster, or a place for kids who are still growing into the sport. The word is used loosely. Some developmental teams are genuinely instructional. Others are a second roster with a full travel calendar.

Ask what the week actually looks like, and how playing time and movement between teams work.

</Card>

<Card title="Rec" id="rec">

Short for recreational. Usually a local league run through a city, a Y, or a parks and rec department. Kids are often placed by age or a light evaluation, not a high-stakes tryout. Seasons are shorter, travel is mostly across town, and playing time is more even.

Some programs call a team “rec” when it already travels and holds tryouts. If that matters to your family, ask. See [Start here](/start-here).

</Card>

<Card title="School-adjacent" id="school-adjacent">

A school team, a booster-run program, or a club organized around the school season. The question is how it works with school sports, who coaches, and what the year looks like when school ball is out of season.

[Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) has a fuller comparison.

</Card>

<Card title="Showcase" id="showcase">

Usually an event, not a team. The pitch is exposure: college coaches, recruiting profiles, measured times and skills. Families pay entry fees and travel for a weekend, or a series of them.

Some showcases are worthwhile; others mainly collect fees. A showcase is not a substitute for a good regular program, and it’s rarely the right first step for a younger child. See [Start here](/start-here).

</Card>

<Card title="Travel / travel team" id="travel">

A team that regularly leaves town for tournaments or games. Tryouts are common. The season can run longer, weekends get spoken for, and costs jump because of tournament fees, hotels, gas, and extra gear.

“Travel ball” is the usual phrase in baseball and softball; other sports may just say a team “travels.” See [Start here](/start-here).

</Card>

</Cards>

## Joining a team

<Cards>

<Card title="Commitment" id="commitment">

Saying yes to a team for a season, or longer. An offer can feel like a compliment. A commitment is a calendar, a cost, and a set of expectations.

You do not have to accept in the parking lot. If a program will not give a family a short window to talk it through at home, that is information. See [Tryouts](/tryouts), and [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) before you pay a deposit.

</Card>

<Card title="Deposit" id="deposit">

Money you pay to hold a roster spot. It is often due quickly after an offer, and it may not be refundable.

A deposit should come after you have a clear picture of the schedule, the cost, and the program — not before you’ve seen a practice or gotten those answers. See [Costs](/costs) and [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program).

</Card>

<Card title="Evaluation" id="evaluation">

A look at a player’s skills, sometimes lighter than a formal tryout. Rec leagues may use a short evaluation to form teams. Clubs may call a tryout an evaluation, or ask your child to come to practice instead.

The name is less important than the format. Ask what the day will look like, what the coaches are looking at, and when you’ll hear back. See [Tryouts](/tryouts).

</Card>

<Card title="Guest playing" id="guest-playing">

Playing a game or a tournament with a team your child is not fully rostered on, usually to fill a gap or give a player extra games. Rules vary by league and by sport: some allow it freely, some limit it, and some treat it as a tryout in disguise.

Ask who is in charge, what it costs, and whether it creates any obligation to that team.

</Card>

<Card title="Offer" id="offer">

An invitation to join a roster. It can feel like a relief, and then immediately like a countdown.

A yes is a season, not a compliment. Before you pay a deposit, you still get to look at the schedule, the cost, and whether this is the program you thought it was. See [Tryouts](/tryouts).

</Card>

<Card title="Release" id="release">

Permission, written or otherwise, for a player to leave one program and join another. Whether you need one, and who has to sign it, depends on the league or the club.

Ask about this before you assume you can just walk away. [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program) includes what to ask if your child is injured, needs to miss for school, or wants to leave mid-season.

</Card>

<Card title="Roster / rostering" id="roster">

The official list of players on a team. “Rostering” is the paperwork and the decision of putting a child on that list — sometimes with a league registration, an age-group or birth-year confirmation, and limits on how many teams they can be on at once.

A roster spot is what a tryout is usually deciding. Ask what being on the roster commits you to, and whether your child can play elsewhere the same season.

</Card>

<Card title="Tryout" id="tryout">

How a program decides who is on a team. That can be one evening of drills and a scrimmage, a weekend, or a few sessions in a row. Some programs call it an evaluation, ask your child to come to practice, or add a second session or a callback.

You should be told, in plain language, what the day will look like, what the coaches are looking at, and when you’ll hear back. The [Tryouts](/tryouts) chapter is the full walkthrough.

</Card>

</Cards>

## The season and the events

<Cards>

<Card title="Age group / birth year" id="age-group">

How a program decides which team a child is eligible for. Some group kids by birth year, some by a cutoff date, and some by school grade. The same child can be “eligible” for different teams depending on the rule.

Always ask how this program groups players, and whether a child can play up or must play down. Don’t assume the team name matches your child’s grade or birthday.

</Card>

<Card title="Bracket" id="bracket">

The elimination side of a tournament, after pool play. Wins and losses here usually decide how far a team goes, and they can add games — or an early trip home.

Ask how a specific tournament is structured, and whether a consolation or placement bracket means more games after a loss.

</Card>

<Card title="Burnout" id="burnout">

Not a diagnosis you have to make at home. It is a pattern worth noticing, in kids and in parents: dread before practice, a joy that has gone flat, lingering aches, a steady “I have to” where “I want to” used to be — or, in a parent, resentment in the car and the feeling that you can’t stop because you’ve already paid.

One off week is a long season. A stretch of weeks like this is a reason to talk. See [Family life and burnout](/family-life-and-burnout).

</Card>

<Card title="Exposure" id="exposure">

The idea that college coaches, scouts, or other programs will see your child play. Showcases sell it. Some clubs and tournaments do too.

Exposure is not automatic, and it is not a good reason by itself to sign a younger child up. If a program leans hard on scholarships or being “the only path,” slow down. See [Start here](/start-here) and the red flags in [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program).

</Card>

<Card title="Level labels (elite, premier, select)" id="level-labels">

Words programs use to describe how competitive a team is. They are marketing as often as they are a standard. “Select,” “premier,” “elite,” “gold,” and “silver” do not mean the same thing from one club or sport to the next.

Ask what the week, the travel, and the expectations actually are, rather than trusting the adjective.

</Card>

<Card title="Off-season" id="off-season">

A stretch of the year when the team is not in full season — or shouldn’t be. Year-round play is a choice some families make. It is not a requirement for a child to be serious.

An off-season, a second sport, or some weeks of unstructured play can be a sign of a healthy program, not a lack of commitment. See [Family life and burnout](/family-life-and-burnout).

</Card>

<Card title="Playing time" id="playing-time">

How much of a game or a season your child is actually on the field, the court, or the pitch. Programs decide this in different ways, and they are not always clear about it up front.

A good coach can explain how playing time works, and how you’ll hear about it. If answers are vague or shifting, that is information. See [Choosing a program](/choosing-a-program).

</Card>

<Card title="Pool play" id="pool-play">

The first part of many tournaments, where teams play a set of games in a group. Results usually seed the bracket that follows. Pool games still count, but they are not always sudden-death.

Ask how many games are guaranteed, and what happens after the pool.

</Card>

<Card title="Tournament" id="tournament">

A weekend (or longer) event where teams play a set of games, often away from home. It is a major source of travel, cost, and time. A tournament is not only the games. It’s the packing, the hotel, the hours on the road, and the way the rest of the household has to orbit that weekend.

Ask how many tournaments are already on the calendar, how far they are, and how often that list grows after families commit. See [Costs](/costs) and [Family life and burnout](/family-life-and-burnout).

</Card>

<Card title="Year-round" id="year-round">

A program or a habit of playing the sport in every season, with little or no break. Some clubs are built this way. It is not the only way to get better.

If a program treats rest as disloyalty, or fills every gap with a required extra, take that seriously before you sign up again. See [Family life and burnout](/family-life-and-burnout).

</Card>

</Cards>

## Money

[Costs](/costs) is the chapter that walks through a season. These are the words that show up on flyers and in follow-up emails.

<Cards>

<Card title="Buyout" id="buyout">

A fee you can pay instead of participating in fundraising. Not every program offers one.

Ask whether fundraising is required, what the expected amount of selling or buying is, and whether a buyout exists. See [Costs](/costs).

</Card>

<Card title="Extras" id="extras">

The costs that rarely appear on the flyer: fundraising or a buyout, team photos, banquets, required lessons or extra skills sessions, coach gifts, and tournaments added late in the season. Each one can feel small. Together they are not.

If more than one child plays, add each schedule up separately. See [Costs](/costs).

</Card>

<Card title="Fundraising" id="fundraising">

Selling, soliciting, or working events to raise money for the team or club — or a required buy-in if you don’t. It is easy to treat as optional until it isn’t.

Ask what is expected, and whether there’s a buyout, before you commit.

</Card>

<Card title="Gear" id="gear">

Equipment, shoes or cleats, bags, training gear, and replacements as kids grow, sometimes mid-season. Some programs require a specific brand, a second uniform, or extra training gear.

This is beyond what the team orders as a uniform package. See [Costs](/costs).

</Card>

<Card title="Pay-to-play" id="pay-to-play">

A simple way to describe how many travel and club programs work: families pay fees to be on the team, rather than joining a free school or rec option. Paying does not guarantee playing time, a particular coach, or a particular result.

Ask what’s included in the fee, and what usually gets added later.

</Card>

<Card title="Private lessons" id="private-lessons">

One-on-one or small-group instruction, paid separately from the team, often with a coach or a trainer. Some programs require lessons or extra skills sessions; many families add them on their own.

Required lessons are a cost, and they are also more time. Ask whether they are optional. See [Costs](/costs) and [Family life and burnout](/family-life-and-burnout).

</Card>

<Card title="Team fees / club fees" id="team-fees">

The number on the flyer: club or travel dues, league fees, and the “uniform package.” This is the part everyone sees first.

Ask what’s actually included: how many jerseys, and whether tournament entries are extra. “Club fees,” “dues,” and “registration” are often this same bucket, or pieces of it. See [Costs](/costs).

</Card>

<Card title="Tournament fees" id="tournament-fees">

What it costs to enter a tournament. Sometimes these are included in the team fee; often they are billed separately, or added when a new event appears on the calendar.

Ask whether tournament entries are included, how many are already planned, and who decides if more get added. See [Costs](/costs).

</Card>

</Cards>

## Safety

[Safety](/safety) is the chapter for what to watch for, ask about, and expect. A few terms come up often enough to define here.

<Cards>

<Card title="Background check" id="background-check">

A screening some programs run on coaches, assistants, and sometimes drivers or chaperones. What is checked, how often, and who is included varies.

Ask who is included and how often it happens. A check is not the same thing as a culture you can see. See [Safety](/safety).

</Card>

<Card title="Chaperone" id="chaperone">

An adult responsible for kids on a trip, at a hotel, or during unsupervised stretches of a weekend. It may be a parent, a coach, or a volunteer.

Ask how rooms, curfews, and supervision are handled on overnight trips, and who is actually in charge at two in the morning. See [Safety](/safety).

</Card>

<Card title="Coach-athlete boundaries" id="coach-athlete-boundaries">

The line between a close, demanding, or kind coach and contact that isn’t appropriate. In a well-run program, one-on-one time in a closed room, a car, or a locker room is not the normal way of doing business. Messages to a child are not a private channel that cuts parents out.

You should be able to get a plain answer about the program’s rules for contact, messaging, locker rooms, and travel. See [Safety](/safety).

</Card>

<Card title="Concussion" id="concussion">

A head injury. A bump, blow, or jolt to the head — or a hit to the body that snaps the head — is a reason to sit your child out and contact a health care provider. Do not let them “shake it off” and return the same day on a hunch.

The CDC’s HEADS UP pages have parent-level information on concussions: [https\://www\.cdc.gov/heads-up/index.html](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/index.html). They can help you understand what to watch for. They do not clear a child to play. A health care provider does that. See [Safety](/safety).

</Card>

<Card title="Protocol" id="protocol">

The program’s written plan for what happens after an injury, in the heat, or after a possible concussion — including who is allowed to sit a child out, and how return to practice or games is decided.

You do not need a coach’s permission to get your child checked. Return should follow the doctor and the protocol, not a good half-hour later. See [Safety](/safety).

</Card>

<Card title="SafeSport" id="safesport">

The U.S. Center for SafeSport publishes training and policies used across Olympic and Paralympic sport, and some programs outside that system use the same training or similar rules. Ask what your program actually requires.

The Center’s site is [https\://uscenterforsafesport.org/](https://uscenterforsafesport.org/). A certificate on a wall is not the same thing as a culture you can see, and not every club or travel team falls under the Center’s formal process. See [Safety](/safety).

</Card>

</Cards>
