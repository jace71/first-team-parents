## The kinds of programs you’ll see

[Start here](/start-here) maps rec, travel, club, and showcase. This table keeps those in view and adds two more you’ll run into: academy programs, and programs that sit next to a school team. The point is not the label. It’s what you’re actually signing up for.

<Compare>

| Type | What you’re usually signing up for | The question to ask | Often a better fit when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rec | A local, season-by-season league, usually close to home | Whether this is truly rec, or a “rec” team that already travels and holds tryouts | Your child is starting out, or your family wants the sport without a year-round calendar |
| Travel | A team that regularly leaves town to compete; tryouts are common, and weekends get spoken for | How many weekends are away, how far, and whether that list is still growing | Your child already loves the sport and wants more, and your family can live with the calendar |
| Club | An organized program, often with paid coaches and regular training; it can be year-round | What this specific team actually does — some clubs also run rec or developmental sides | Your child is ready for structured training and a longer commitment, not just a sharper-looking uniform |
| Academy | A training-focused setup, sometimes attached to a club or a facility; it may be a team, a training track, or mostly classes | Whether it’s a team, a training program, or both — and what “academy” means here | You want more instruction, and you’ve confirmed it isn’t simply a more expensive practice schedule |
| School-adjacent | A school team, a booster-run program, or a club organized around the school season | How it works with school sports, who coaches, and what the year looks like when school ball is out of season | The school calendar is already your family’s anchor, or your child wants both school and club and you need to see if that’s actually possible |

<Note>These are general patterns. Programs use the words differently, so always ask what a specific team means by them. Showcase is usually an event, not a team; that’s covered in [Start here](/start-here).</Note>

</Compare>

## Coaches and culture

The name on the jersey matters less than the person your child will hear from several days a week, and the atmosphere around that person. You can learn a lot before you commit if you watch a practice and listen to how questions are answered.

<Cards>

<Card title="The coach" label="The person your child will hear from most" id="the-coach">

Watch how they talk to players when a mistake happens, not only when something goes well. A good coach can explain how playing time works, will let you watch a practice, and can answer a direct question without making you feel like a problem.

<Ask>If you can, watch a practice before you pay a deposit.</Ask>

</Card>

<Card title="The culture" label="What it feels like to be on this team" id="the-culture">

Notice how parents behave on the sideline, and how the staff responds. Kids should be able to make a mistake without being humiliated. Winning can matter without being the only story the program tells.

</Card>

<Card title="How they talk to families" label="Clarity is a good sign" id="clarity">

A well-run program can tell you the weekly time, the travel, what’s included, and how decisions get made. If the only answers you get are pressure, slogans, or “trust us,” believe that.

</Card>

</Cards>

## Fit for your kid, and for your family

A program can be well run and still be wrong for your household.

Time is more than the practice hours on the flyer. It’s weekday evenings, early departures, packing, and who else in the house has to rearrange. Travel is not only distance; it’s how often you’ll be gone, and whether those weekends land on top of school, work, or another child’s games.

If more than one child plays, add each schedule up separately. Two “reasonable” teams can still make a weekend that doesn’t work. [Costs](/costs) walks through the money. [Family life and burnout](/family-life-and-burnout) will go further on the calendar. For now, write the week down as it would actually look.

The other half of fit is your child. A kid who lights up at practice and a kid who is mainly trying to keep up with friends, or with an adult’s plan, are in different situations — even on the same roster.

## Talking with your child

Have the conversation at home, when you’re both reasonably calm — not in the car leaving a tryout, and not in a parking lot full of other parents.

Ask what they like about this, and what they’re nervous about. Listen all the way through before you start solving. You can share concerns about time, travel, or a coach without turning the talk into a verdict on their talent.

It’s allowed to wait a season. It’s allowed to stay in rec. A child who feels heard now is easier to trust later, including if they want to stop. The question from [Start here](/start-here) still applies: whose idea is this — theirs, or the adults’?

## Questions to ask before you commit

You don’t need a long interrogation. You do need clear answers to a few plain questions. If a program can’t or won’t answer them, that is information.

<Checklist>

- Who is the actual coach for this team, and can we watch a practice?
- How is playing time decided, and how will we hear about it?
- What does a typical week look like, including travel days?
- How many weekends are away, and how often does that list grow after families commit?
- What’s included in the fee, and what usually gets added later? Use the list on [Costs](/costs) if it’s helpful.
- What happens if our child is injured, needs to miss for school, or wants to leave mid-season?
- How does this program handle other sports, school events, or a sibling’s schedule?
- Can we talk with a current parent — and choose that person ourselves, not only a name we’re handed?

</Checklist>

## Red flags

One of these is a reason to slow down. Several together are a reason to walk away.

- A hard push to pay a deposit before you’ve seen a practice, or before you’ve received a clear picture of the schedule and the cost.
- Vague or shifting answers about playing time, fees, or how many weekends you’ll be gone.
- A coach or director who won’t let families watch, or who treats questions as disloyalty.
- Big promises about exposure, scholarships, or being “the only path,” especially for a younger child.
- A practice or sideline where kids seem afraid, or where parents warn you not to ask questions.
- A program that needs to badmouth every other option in town in order to make its case.
